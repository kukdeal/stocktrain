From candlesticks to Fibonacci · A beginner's operating manual · 中文版 ↗

Stack five tools into one method you can actually trade

Technical analysis is not five disconnected topics — it is a ladder climbed in order: candlesticks tell you who won today, moving averages give you direction, trendlines draw the map, Elliott Wave supplies the rhythm, and Fibonacci turns "roughly there" into numbers. This handbook covers the theory in that order, then adds the parts that actually decide your P&L — a step-by-step trading procedure, a set of money-management rules, and a review toolkit.

How to use this handbook

Three reading conventions

  • Colors: this handbook follows the Western convention — green = up (bullish), red = down (bearish). Charts in mainland China use the opposite (red up, green down), so confirm the color scheme before trusting your instincts on an unfamiliar chart.
  • Market-agnostic: only the universal principles of technical analysis are covered here. Where trading rules differ across markets (T+0 vs T+1 settlement, price limits, session hours), the text flags it — your own market's rules take precedence.
  • Picture first, then text: every section carries a diagram. Study the figure and guess what it is saying before reading the prose — that is exactly the exercise your brain performs in front of a live chart.
HOW TO PRACTICE

Reading without drilling teaches nothing. The most effective drill is bar replay: rewind a chart three years in your trading software, then step forward one bar at a time — before each new bar, write down "what would I do right now", then reveal it. Thirty bars a day for two weeks builds genuine chart sense — worth more than ten books.

LADDER 01 · THE MOTHER OF TECHNICAL ANALYSISGoal: read what a single candle is saying

Candlestick charts — the language of price

Candlestick charts originated in eighteenth-century Japan, in the rice markets of the Tokugawa shogunate, as a way to record the daily rise and fall of rice prices. Because one symbol packs in four prices plus the tug-of-war between buyers and sellers, the method was later adopted by securities markets — and three hundred years on it remains the common language of stocks, futures, and FX.

The chart has exactly one basic purpose: finding entry and exit points. Every fancier tool is an annotation built on top of it.

1.1 The four elements of one candle

A single candle is built from the open, close, high, and low. Close above open makes a bullish candle; close below open makes a bearish candle. The thin lines above and below the body are wicks (shadows) — a record of prices the market visited during the day but failed to hold.

1.2 Measuring bull vs bear strength

The four elements let you read the day's balance of power directly:

Bull strength = Close − Low // how far price was lifted off the day's bottom
Bear strength = High − Close // how far price was knocked down from the day's top

Which yields the conclusion beginners most often miss: a bullish candle does not prove the bulls were stronger, and a bearish candle does not prove the bears were.

1.3 A single candle only means something inside a trend

One candle describes one day's battle. To trade it, you must put it back into context:

  • Candle agrees with the trend: the two "resonate," and continuation in the trend direction is the higher-probability outcome — the basis for adding to winners.
  • Candle disagrees with the trend: first ask whether the trend structure is actually broken. A bear-dominated candle inside an uptrend is usually a pause or a shakeout; a bull-dominated candle inside a downtrend is usually just a bounce.

1.4 Common candlestick patterns

The second step is reading groups of candles. Patterns fall into five families: bottoming (hammer, morning star), topping (shooting star, evening star), bullish/bearish reversals (engulfing, harami), continuation (triangles, flags), and gaps (up-gaps, down-gaps).

COMMON MISTAKE

The classic beginner error is memorizing dozens of pattern names while ignoring where the pattern occurs. Every bottoming pattern requires a preceding decline; every topping pattern requires a preceding rally. A pattern divorced from location is a lab result divorced from the patient's history.

LADDER 02 · THE TREND SMOOTHERGoal: see direction, support, and resistance at a glance

Moving averages — grinding out the noise

Individual candles jump around; watch too many and the noise carries you away. The moving average (MA) does something humble: add up the last N closes, divide by N, plot one such point per day, connect the dots.

That line is the market's average cost over the period. Everyone who bought during those N days paid, on average, that price — which is why MAs so often act as support or resistance: when price returns to the MA, it returns to the crowd's cost basis.

2.1 Choosing your periods

ClassPeriodNicknamePrimary use
ShortMA5 / MA10weekly / two-week lineEntry and exit timing for swing trades
MediumMA20 / MA30monthly lineHealth check for the medium trend; the holding lifeline
LongMA60 / MA120 / MA250quarterly / half-year / yearlyBull–bear regime, big-picture direction, institutional cost proxy

A beginner does not need eight lines on the chart. Three suffice: one short (MA5 or MA10) for entries, one medium (MA20) for holding, one long (MA60) for direction.

2.2 Golden cross and death cross

A shorter MA crossing up through a longer MA is a golden cross — conventionally a buy signal. Crossing down is a death cross — conventionally a sell.

The routinely forgotten precondition: a golden cross only counts when both MAs are flat or rising; a death cross only counts when both are flat or falling. A "golden cross" printed while both lines still plunge is just a bounce inside a decline, not a buy point.

2.3 Bullish and bearish alignment

When short, medium, and long MAs stack top-to-bottom in that order (MA5 > MA10 > MA20 > MA60), all rising, that is bullish alignment — the bulls own the field; lean long and hold. The mirror image (long on top, all falling) is bearish alignment — stand aside or lean short.

2.4 MA convergence — the silence before the move

When two or more MAs braid around each other with no clear direction, they are converged. Convergence means cost bases across timeframes have equalized and disagreement is minimal — a state that never lasts. Convergence is typically resolved by a directional breakout.

2.5 Two quantitative gauges: slope and bias

SLOPE MA slope

The angle between an MA and the horizontal. Empirically, trends holding a 30°–45° slope sustain best: flatter lacks momentum, steeper (>60°) is borrowed from the future and prone to sharp snap-backs.

BIAS Bias ratio

Price's percentage distance from an MA: BIAS = (Close − MA) ÷ MA × 100%. The wider the bias, the likelier the snap back to the MA — large positive bias warns against chasing; large negative bias hints at a bounce.

2.6 Granville's eight rules

Granville's rules reduce "price versus its MA" to eight situations — four buys and four sells. It is the classic, most learnable MA playbook.

BUY B1First cross above

The MA turns from falling to flat or rising, and price crosses up through it for the first time. Possible trend reversal — the most important first buy.

BUY B2Dip below, reclaim

In an uptrend price slips under the MA while the MA keeps rising, then reclaims it.

BUY B3Pullback holds the MA

Price falls back to the MA, touches it, and turns up without a real break. The most comfortable with-trend add point.

BUY B4Deep negative bias + bullish pattern

Price stretched far below the MA (oversold), with a hammer or bullish engulfing printing. A bounce trade — keep it small.

SELL S1First break below

After a big rally the MA flattens or rolls over and price breaks down through it. The first sell.

SELL S2Pop above, fail again

In a downtrend price briefly stands above the MA while the MA keeps falling, then loses it again.

SELL S3Bounce rejected at the MA

Price rallies from below into the MA and gets rejected. The textbook trim point in a downtrend.

SELL S4Deep positive bias + bearish pattern

Price stretched far above the MA (overbought), with a shooting star or bearish engulfing printing. A take-profit signal.

PRACTICAL NOTE

You do not need all eight. Beginners should trade only B1, B3, S1, S3 — the four with the clearest direction. B2/S2 live in choppy territory and B4/S4 are counter-trend; both demand more experience and stricter sizing.

LADDER 03 · THE MAP OF THE MARKETGoal: mark the price levels that matter

Trendlines and support/resistance — drawing the map

Moving averages give you direction, but not where to buy or where to admit you're wrong. Trendlines and support/resistance fill that gap — they turn a noisy chart into a map with marked borders.

3.1 There are only three trends

Judging trend needs no indicator at all — only the relative position of highs and lows:

  • Uptrend: higher highs and higher lows
  • Downtrend: lower highs and lower lows
  • Range: highs and lows oscillating inside a band

3.2 How to draw a trendline

One rule covers it: connect the lows in an uptrend, the highs in a downtrend.

  • Rising trendline (support): connect two or more swing lows of an advancing leg and extend right — an upward-sloping line. Pullbacks into it tend to find support.
  • Falling trendline (resistance): connect two or more swing highs of a declining leg and extend right. Bounces into it tend to stall.

The third point is what matters: two points define a line, the third touch confirms it. A two-point trendline is something anyone can draw anywhere — it proves nothing yet.

DRAWING RULES

Four rules for drawing: ① no candle body may cut through the line between its anchor points; ② at high price levels prefer a log-scale chart, otherwise equal percentage moves look stretched and tilt the line; ③ the longer the timeframe (weekly > daily > hourly), the more reliable the line; ④ a slope near 45° is healthiest — very steep lines break soon.

3.3 Support and resistance

Support is a price area where a decline tends to stall and stabilize — the "floor," a zone of waiting demand. Resistance is where a rally tends to stall and roll over — the "ceiling," a zone of waiting supply.

The core logic in one line: a level that price repeatedly fails to get through becomes, by that very fact, an obstacle.

RULE 01It's a zone, not a tick

Support/resistance is always a price band. Expecting exact reversals at an exact decimal only gets your stops wicked out.

RULE 02Break above resistance → likely to run

A valid break means the supply parked there has been absorbed; price tends to continue higher.

RULE 03Break below support → likely to fall

A valid break of support that holds for 3+ days without being reclaimed is a serious reversal warning.

3.4 Role reversal: support ↔ resistance

The single most useful principle in this chapter: broken support becomes resistance; broken resistance becomes support.

The reason is human: everyone who bought at resistance and got trapped is desperate to break even — their selling caps the next rally there (resistance). Once a real breakout absorbs them, the same shelf becomes the cost basis of fresh buyers (support).

REAL VS FALSE BREAKOUTS

How to tell a valid break from a fake-out — three filters, act only when at least two pass: ① Volume — breakout-day volume clearly expands (≥1.5× the 5-day average); ② Magnitude — the close clears the level by 3%+, not merely leans on it; ③ Time — price holds beyond the level for 2–3 consecutive sessions. A "breakout" that passes none is usually bait.

LADDER 04 · THE MARKET'S RHYTHMGoal: know roughly where in the cycle you stand

Elliott Wave — five up, three down

Wave theory was proposed by R. N. Elliott in the 1930s. It has three pillars: the shape of the waves, the proportions between waves, and the time between them — with shape mattering most, proportion second, time least.

And the mathematics behind those proportions is exactly the Fibonacci sequence of the next chapter — which is why these two chapters must be studied together.

4.1 The eight-wave cycle

A complete price cycle contains eight waves: five impulse waves with the primary direction (labeled 1–5) and three corrective waves against it (labeled A, B, C). When all eight complete, one cycle ends and the next begins.

4.2 Four basic properties of waves

  • Price alternates between advance and decline — the market's basic breathing.
  • Impulse and correction are the two elementary forms; every other shape is a combination of them.
  • Duration does not change form — a wave may take three days or three years; its structure is invariant.
  • Waves are self-similar: each wave subdivides into smaller waves. A daily-chart wave 3 is itself a full eight-wave cycle on the hourly chart.

4.3 The three iron rules of counting

These are hard rules, not tendencies. If any is violated, your count is wrong and must be redone — the market is never the thing at fault.

4.4 The rule of alternation

If wave 2 corrects in a simple shape (a sharp V pullback), wave 4 will usually correct in a complex one (a long sideways grind, a triangle) — and vice versa.

4.5 Counting waves the right way

  1. Start from the higher timeframe: mark the major swing highs and lows on the weekly or daily first, then subdivide. Starting from the 5-minute chart guarantees getting lost.
  2. Keep a primary count and an alternate: the mature workflow is always "main count + alternate count," with a written price level at which you switch.
  3. Falsify with the three rules — don't force with imagination: if it won't count, it won't count; that itself says wave analysis doesn't apply right now. Switch tools or stand aside.
IMPORTANT

Elliott Wave is not a precision forecasting device but a probabilistic framework. Counting is subjective — ten analysts can produce ten counts, and counts get revised after the fact. Used properly, a count is a script hypothesis for the current market, to be verified or killed by trendlines, MAs, and volume — never buy size purely because "I'm counting this as wave 3."

LADDER 05 · THE MATH OF THE WAVESGoal: turn "roughly there" into actual numbers

Fibonacci — quantifying the targets

The first four rungs answer "direction" and "structure." One question remains: where to take profit, and where to buy the dip? Fibonacci tools answer it — not with an exact tick, but with statistically meaningful price zones.

5.1 The sequence and the golden ratio

The Fibonacci sequence starts from 0 and 1; each number is the sum of the previous two. The ratio of adjacent terms converges to 0.618 — the golden ratio; its reciprocal is roughly 1.618.

5.2 Fibonacci retracement — finding support

Retracement locates where the next pullback is likely to stop inside a completed swing.

How to draw: in an uptrend, click the swing low and drag to the swing high — the tool plots the levels; in a downtrend, high first, then low.

STOP-LOSS TIP

Never place the stop exactly on a Fibonacci level. Everyone can see those prices; stop orders pile up there and one quick wick clears them out before price reverses. Leave a buffer of 5–10 ticks (or 10–20% of the instrument's average daily range) beyond the level.

5.3 Fibonacci extension — finding targets

Retracement answers "where to buy the dip"; extension answers "where to sell the rally": it projects a completed impulse's height forward to estimate where the next leg may reach.

Common levels: 61.8%, 100%, 161.8%. Three anchors: start A → high B → pullback low C; project multiples of AB upward from C.

5.4 Working with Elliott Wave

Fibonacci is the mathematical backbone of wave theory. Together they yield a set of working proportions:

WaveTypical proportionPractical meaning
Wave 2retraces 50%–61.8% of wave 1 (61.8% common)Deep pullbacks are normal here — don't declare the trend dead too early
Wave 3161.8% of wave 1; 261.8% when strongThe main-leg target — the one leg worth full size
Wave 4retraces 23.6%–38.2% of wave 3Shallow and slow — don't wait for wave-2 depth
Wave 5100% of wave 1, or 61.8% of the 1→3 spanThe last leg; volume often diverges — fight and retreat
Waves A / CC ≈ 100% or 161.8% of AReference floor for corrections
USE WITH CARE

① Fibonacci gives probabilistic target zones, not exact entries; ② always cross-check with volume, MAs, and trendlines — only when several independent tools point at the same area (confluence) does confidence rise meaningfully; ③ in small-cap or thinly traded names its reliability drops sharply, because a little money can push price anywhere.

PRACTICE A · FROM READING TO ORDERINGGoal: chain the tools into a repeatable procedure

The full trading SOP

The first five chapters were "how to read." This one is "how to act." However much theory you hold, without a fixed procedure you will still order by feel at the screen — and feel is the fastest known way to lose money.

A complete trade has six stages, each with defined inputs, outputs, and reasons not to act.

STEP 1 · Screening: shrink the market to 20 names

The greatest waste of a beginner's time is rummaging through thousands of tickers daily. A fixed filter set concentrates attention:

LayerFilterWhy
LiquidityDaily turnover above the market median; drop micro-caps and chronically shrinking volumeTechnical analysis degrades in illiquid names; slippage and impact costs grow
Trend statePrice above MA60 + bullish alignment (for longs); the reverse for shorts / avoidHunting only downwind raises the base win rate for free
Clean structureThe chart offers drawable trendlines and clear prior highs/lowsIf no line can be drawn, there is no usable structure — skip it
Suitable volatilityRecent range neither dead nor freakishly expandedToo quiet has no room; too wild wicks out reasonable stops
ExclusionsNames facing major uncertainty: pending events, halts, imminent earningsTechnical analysis cannot price a news shock

Names that pass go into the watchlist; follow only those 10–20. Refresh the list weekly — not daily.

STEP 2 · Direction: timeframes must agree

The same stock can flash opposite signals on different timeframes. The cure is a fixed three-timeframe stack: the large one sets direction, the middle one sets structure, the small one times the entry. Only trade when all three point the same way.

STEP 3 · The entry: wait for confluence, not a feeling

Entry quality equals the number of independent tools pointing at the same price area. Three or more, or no trade:

  • Direction: three timeframes agreeWeekly, daily, hourly aligned; otherwise skip the setup entirely
  • Location: at least three tools in confluenceTrendline / MA / Fibonacci level / former resistance-turned-support / round number — any three or more overlapping
  • Signal: an explicit candle confirmationHammer, bullish engulfing, morning star, etc.; location without a signal = still falling
  • Volume: dries up on the pullback, expands on the turnContraction while correcting, expansion on the confirmation day
  • Risk: a defined stop at an acceptable distanceStop goes beyond the confluence zone plus buffer; if that's too far away, this is not your entry
  • Reward: R:R of at least 2:1Target from the extension or prior high, divided by stop distance; below 2:1, pass

STEPS 4–5 · Entry, adds, and exits

Here is the whole rule set laid onto one real-shaped trade:

ENTRYOpen small, add later

The first tranche is at most half the planned size. Add only at the retest, only after price confirms; never add to a losing position.

STOPThe stop goes in with the order

The stop level is fixed before the order is sent (beyond the confluence zone plus buffer). An unstopped trade hands your loss ceiling to luck.

TRAILTrailing stop: up only

After price makes a new structural high, raise the stop under the latest valid swing low. A trailing stop moves only in your favor — never back down.

EXITScale out, don't snipe

Sell a third at target 1, a third at target 2, trail the rest. Give up the fantasy of selling the top.

STEP 6 · Review (see part C)

The review is the only stage that makes you better. Tools in C · Practice & review.

PRACTICE B · SURVIVE FIRST, COMPOUND SECONDGoal: make sure no single trade can hurt you

Money management and risk control

Technical analysis sets your win rate; money management decides whether you survive long enough for the win rate to matter. Most accounts don't die of a wrong call — they die of one oversized wrong call.

B.1 The asymmetry of loss and recovery

Every risk rule starts from this fact: losses and recoveries are not symmetric. The deeper the loss, the faster the required recovery gain accelerates.

DrawdownGain needed to recoverDifficulty
−10%+11.1%One normal swing
−20%+25.0%Needs a decent rally
−30%+42.9%Needs a full advance
−50%+100%Needs a double; most never see it
−70%+233%Effectively starting over
−90%+900%Academic

B.2 The per-trade risk rule: size from the stop

Beginners think "how much should I buy?" first. Professionals invert it: fix "how much am I willing to lose on this trade," then derive the share count.

Max loss per trade = account equity × per-trade risk (suggested 1%, never above 2%)
Stop distance per share = entry price − stop price
Shares = max loss per trade ÷ stop distance per share

B.3 Thinking in R: reward ratio and expectancy

Call the max loss per trade 1R and measure all outcomes in R: a $2,000 win is +2R, a $1,000 loss is −1R. Now every trade is comparable regardless of account size or share price.

Expectancy = win rate × avg win (R) − (1 − win rate) × avg loss (R)
e.g. 40% win rate, 3:1 payoff → 0.4 × 3 − 0.6 × 1 = +0.6R per trade: positive long-run edge
Payoff ratioBreakeven win rateExpectancy @ 40% winsExpectancy @ 50% wins
1 : 150.0%−0.20R0.00R

The counter-intuitive takeaway: you can be profitable with a 40% win rate — if the payoff ratio is high enough. Conversely, someone winning 70% of trades at 1:3 (quick small wins, stubborn big losses) is guaranteed to bleed out.

SCREENING RULE

Use the payoff ratio as a pre-entry filter: compute stop distance and distance to target 1; below 2:1, walk away. This one rule alone deletes most impulse trades.

B.4 Three ways to set a stop

STRUCTURALStructural stop (preferred)

Beyond the latest valid swing low, trendline, or confluence zone plus a buffer. Cleanest logic: if price gets there, the reason for the trade is gone.

VOLATILITYVolatility stop

Set from average true range, e.g. entry − 2×ATR. Suits mixed portfolios; keeps normal noise from tagging your stop.

TIMETime stop

If the trade hasn't moved as expected within N sessions, exit regardless of P&L. A capital-efficiency tool; the cure for slow-boiled positions.

NEVER

The percentage stop ("out at −10%") is the worst kind — 10% has nothing to do with market structure: it triggers inside normal noise or sits uselessly beyond the real level. The chart sets the stop; the stop sets the size — never the other way around.

B.5 Three portfolio-level ceilings

  • Per-trade risk ≤ 1% for beginners; 2% once consistent — never more.
  • Total open risk ≤ 3–5%: even if every open stop fires at once, the drawdown stays survivable.
  • Correlation control: names in the same sector or driven by the same story count as one position. Five EV stocks aren't diversification — they're one bet, five times the size.

Plus one psychological circuit breaker: at a 6–8% monthly drawdown, cut size or stop trading for a week. Judgment is worst during losing streaks; what you need then is a pause, not a comeback.

EXTEND D · THE INDICATOR LIBRARYGoal: know the algorithm, so you never worship the indicator

15 major indicators: algorithms and usage

Indicators are not oracles — each is a piece of arithmetic run on price and volume. Once you know the arithmetic, you know what a given indicator can answer and what it cannot; most "the indicator failed" complaints are really "I asked it a question it never computes."

Three families by purpose: trend-following (MA, EMA, MACD, DMI, SAR, the Bollinger midline) tracks direction; oscillators (KDJ, RSI, W%R, CCI, BIAS, DPO) measure overbought/oversold; volume/volatility (VOL, OBV, ATR, Bollinger bandwidth) measures participation and range. Trend tools fail in ranges; oscillators fail in trends — the first rule of choosing.

D.1 The MA family: SMA, EMA, WMA

Chapter 2's MA is the simple moving average (SMA) — equal weight to every day. Its cost: slow reactions, plus phantom jumps when old data drops out of the window. Two refinements:

SMA(N) = (C₁ + C₂ + … + C_N) ÷ N // equal weights
WMA(N) = (N·C₁ + (N−1)·C₂ + … + 1·C_N) ÷ (N+…+1) // linearly more weight to recent days
EMA(N): today's EMA = α·close + (1−α)·yesterday's EMA, α = 2/(N+1) // exponential decay; old data never fully leaves
e.g. EMA12: α = 2/13 ≈ 0.154 — today's price gets 15.4% weight; 84.6% carries over from yesterday's EMA

Usage difference: EMA hugs price closer and turns faster — better for short-term work; SMA is calmer and less noisy — better for position trades. MACD is built entirely from EMAs. Don't mix both types in one crossover system, or the signals will contradict each other.

D.2 MACD — king of indicators

MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence) measures the gap between a fast and a slow EMA, and how that gap changes — an accelerometer for the trend.

DIF (fast line) = EMA12 − EMA26 // short-term momentum relative to long-term
DEA (signal line) = 9-day EMA of DIF // DIF smoothed once more, as the trigger baseline
Histogram = 2 × (DIF − DEA) // above zero and below; its growth and shrinkage is momentum itself
SIGNAL 1Crosses

DIF crossing above DEA is bullish, below is bearish. A bullish cross above the zero line (pullback ending inside an uptrend) far outweighs one below it (a bounce inside a decline).

SIGNAL 2Zero-line position

DIF and DEA above zero = EMA12 above EMA26 = bull regime. Long braiding around zero = MA convergence: wait for direction.

SIGNAL 3Histogram breathing

Bars lengthening = acceleration; bars shortening = fading momentum (price may still rise). The histogram's turn often leads price's turn.

SIGNAL 4Divergence (the big one)

Top/bottom divergence is MACD's most reliable use — and the standard tool for Chan Theory's beichi (chapter E). Divergence can repeat several times; always wait for the candle confirmation.

D.3 KDJ — the stochastic

KDJ answers: where does today's close sit inside the last N days' range? (N is usually 9.)

RSV = (Close − N-day Low) ÷ (N-day High − N-day Low) × 100 // 0 = on the floor of the range, 100 = on its ceiling
K = ⅔ × K_prev + ⅓ × RSV // smoothed RSV
D = ⅔ × D_prev + ⅓ × K // K smoothed again
J = 3K − 2D // amplifies K's deviation from D; most sensitive, can leave 0–100
PINNING

KDJ's biggest trap is pinning: in a one-way trend it can ride the overbought band for weeks, making every "overbought sell" wrong. KDJ belongs in ranges and in timing the end of pullbacks — never as a counter-trend trigger inside a runaway trend. Decide trend-vs-range first, with MA alignment (ch. 2) and ADX (D.7).

D.4 RSI — relative strength index

RSI(N) = average N-day gain ÷ (average gain + average loss) × 100 // N commonly 6, 12, 14, 24

Reading: 50 divides bull from bear; above 70 overbought, below 30 oversold (80/20 in strong markets). Like KDJ but smoother — less pinning, better as confirmation: RSI holding above 50 confirms a bullish structure; RSI divergence stacking with MACD divergence is a heavyweight topping signal.

D.5 BOLL — Bollinger Bands

Bollinger Bands use the statistical standard deviation to draw "how far is price from its average" directly on the chart:

Middle band MB = 20-day SMA
Upper band UP = MB + 2 × σ(20) // σ = std-dev of the last 20 closes
Lower band DN = MB − 2 × σ(20) // ~95% of prices fall inside the bands, statistically

D.6 DPO — detrended price oscillator

DPO does something unusual: it subtracts the trend from price, leaving only the cyclical wiggle.

DPO = Close − SMA(N) shifted back (N/2 + 1) days // N usually 20: close minus the MA20 from 11 days ago
Shifting the MA half a cycle before subtracting cancels the long trend; what's left oscillates around zero

D.8 Principles of combining indicators

  • First classify the regime with ADX or MA alignment: trending → MACD + MAs + SAR; ranging → KDJ/RSI + Bollinger extremes.
  • Only cross-family combinations add information: MACD + KDJ + RSI together tell you little (all price-derived, highly correlated); MACD (trend) + KDJ (oscillator) + OBV (volume) are three genuinely independent views.
  • Indicators lag or coincide with price — always: they are functions of price and contain no future. Their value is making price information standardized and comparable, not prophetic.
  • Three indicators per screen, maximum. More never makes you righter — it only guarantees you can always find the one that agrees with you.
EXTEND E · STRUCTURAL ANALYSISGoal: grasp the recursion fractal → stroke → segment → pivot

Chan Theory — the geometry of price moves

Chan Theory (缠论, "Chanlun") is the most influential original technical framework to come out of the Chinese-speaking world. Its key difference from Elliott Wave: waves are counted (subjectively), while Chan builds everything bottom-up from strict definitions — starting at candle inclusion and constructing fractals, strokes, segments, and pivots, each step with an explicit test.

The core idea in one line: every move at every level must complete itself — a move is either a "trend" or a "consolidation," each rigorously defined by the number and position of its pivots, and a completed move must hand off to the next.

E.1 From candles to strokes: inclusion and fractals

Step 1 — inclusion processing: when one of two adjacent candles fully contains the other's range, merge them in the direction of travel (rising: take the higher high and higher low; falling: lower low and lower high). Only an inclusion-free sequence can define fractals.

Step 2 — fractals: among three adjacent candles, if the middle one has the highest high and the highest low, that is a top fractal; the mirror image is a bottom fractal.

Step 3 — strokes: a top fractal and a bottom fractal separated by at least one independent candle (≥5 candles total including both fractals) connect into a stroke — Chan's smallest unit of movement.

E.2 The pivot (zhongshu) — Chan's heart

The pivot is defined as the overlap of at least three consecutive sub-level segments. Take the first three: pivot top ZG = the lowest of their highs; pivot bottom ZD = the highest of their lows.

A pivot is the zone where bulls and bears fight to a temporary standstill — and once formed, it becomes both a gravity well and the classification yardstick for what follows:

  • Consolidation: the whole move contains one pivot.
  • Trend: two or more pivots in the same direction (each higher = uptrend; each lower = downtrend).

E.4 Beichi — measuring exhaustion

Beichi (背驰, "divergence/exhaustion") means a new leg makes a new high (or low) with less force than the previous comparable leg. The standard force gauge is MACD: compare the two legs' histogram areas (or DIF peak heights) — a clearly smaller area on the newer leg is beichi. This is the same phenomenon as D.2's MACD divergence, but Chan adds stricter comparability conditions:

  • Only two legs of the same level, same direction, separated by the same pivot are comparable — wave 3 vs wave 5 is a valid comparison; legs from different levels are not.
  • Consolidation beichi (two weakening probes around one pivot) is minor — expect only a reaction; trend beichi (after two or more pivots) is what precedes meaningful reversals.
  • Beichi = exhausted momentum ≠ instant reversal. Confirmation is always a sub-level reversal structure (an opposite fractal/stroke) — never the beichi alone.

E.5 Levels and recursion

Chan's "level" is not simply a chart timeframe but a structural recursion: one segment at this level is built from several sub-level segments; a pivot at this level is built from overlapping sub-level segments. The practical shortcut: use 5-min / 30-min / daily / weekly as your operating ladder — find pivots and buy points on the 30-min, confirm beichi and turns on the 5-min, set the campaign direction on the daily. This is the same idea as chapter A's multi-timeframe confluence, expressed structurally.

HOW TO STUDY IT

The full system is vast (the original ran to 108 lessons), with far more definitions than this section covers. A beginner needs exactly four things: ① fractal and stroke marking (trains structural vision); ② pivot identification (locates the battlegrounds); ③ Buy/Sell type 3 (the most tradeable signal); ④ MACD-assisted beichi. Avoid the rabbit hole of segment-division debates — that is the fork between tools serving your trading and trading serving your tools.

EXTEND F · DERIVATIVESGoal: read an option's payoff structure and know where its risks live

Options — strategies, risks, and worked examples

An option is a contract of rights: the buyer pays a premium for the right to buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying at the strike price on (or before) the expiry date — a right, never an obligation. The seller collects the premium and carries the obligation to deliver if assigned.

F.1 Five core concepts

ConceptMeaningKey point
Strike KThe agreed transaction priceIts position versus spot makes the option ITM (in the money), ATM, or OTM
PremiumThe option's price = intrinsic + time valueIntrinsic = what immediate exercise yields; the rest is time value, decaying to zero at expiry
ExpiryWhen the right lapsesAmerican style allows early exercise, European only at expiry; decay accelerates near the end
Implied volatility (IV)The volatility the market's price impliesHigher IV = dearer premium; IV inflates before events and collapses after (IV crush)
MultiplierUnits of underlying per contractUS equity options: 1 contract = 100 shares; all P&L scales by it

F.2 Premium anatomy: ITM, ATM, OTM

Option price = intrinsic value + time value. This decomposition underlies every strategy that follows. With the stock at $100, compare three calls of the same expiry at different strikes:

ContractMoneynessPremiumIntrinsicTime valueCharacter
K = 90 callITM11.5010.001.50Expensive, but mostly "real money"; high delta — trades like the stock
K = 100 callATM4.0004.00Maximum time value — peak uncertainty, so sellers charge the most here
K = 110 callOTM1.2001.20Cheap, highest leverage — but 100% time value, and most likely to expire at zero

Three principles fall straight out of the table:

  • Time value peaks at the money and shrinks toward deep ITM and deep OTM — which is why sellers love writing near-the-money contracts (maximum rent), and why buying deep OTM is buying lottery tickets.
  • Time value only ever flows to the seller: up day or down day, you wake up with a little less of it. A buyer profits only when intrinsic value grows faster than time value bleeds.
  • Volatility is the second price axis: the same contract can double in premium when IV goes from 30% to 60%. Before buying, check where IV sits in its own history — low IV favors buying, high IV favors selling; direction is the second question, not the first.

F.3 The Greeks: four dials of risk

DELTA ΔDirection sensitivity

Option price change per $1 move in the underlying. ATM ≈ 0.5; deep ITM → 1. Also a rough read of "probability of expiring ITM."

THETA ΘTime decay

Time value lost per day. Buyers pay rent on time daily; sellers collect it — fastest in the final 30 days.

VEGA νVolatility sensitivity

Premium change per 1-point IV move. Buying options = long volatility; buy when IV is already rich and you can lose even when right on direction.

GAMMA ΓDelta's acceleration

How fast delta itself changes. Near-expiry ATM options carry huge gamma — small underlying moves flip the P&L violently.

HOW TO CHOOSE A STRATEGY

Don't start from strategy names — start from three judgments: ① direction (up / down / sideways / unknown); ② magnitude (big move or small); ③ IV level (rich or cheap). The combination points straight at the structure: bullish + small move + rich IV → bull spread or covered call; bullish + big move + cheap IV → outright long call; sideways + rich IV → iron condor; unknown direction + big move + cheap IV → straddle/strangle. Rich IV favors seller structures; only cheap IV justifies buying — this single habit removes half of all beginner option losses.

F.6 Six worked examples

Teaching examples with invented round numbers, ignoring fees and taxes. Let stock S trade at $100, multiplier 100.

EXAMPLE 1 · LONG CALL Risking $300 on an earnings pop

Buy 1 call, one month out, K = $105, premium $3 (pay 3 × 100 = $300).

At expiry S = $115: exercise value (115−105) × 100 = $1,000 → net +$700 (+233%).
At expiry S ≤ $105: premium gone, −$300 (−100%).
Buying 100 shares instead needs $10,000: at $115 you'd make $1,500 (+15%); at $90 you'd lose $1,000.

Lesson: options make the small-probability / big-payoff trade explicit — most OTM options expire worthless, so size every buy as if it will go to zero.

EXAMPLE 2 · COVERED CALL Collecting rent on shares you hold

Hold 100 shares (cost $100). Sell 1 call, one month out, K = $110, premium $2 (+$200).

At expiry S = $104: call expires worthless; keep the $200 (≈24% annualized "rent") and the shares.
At expiry S = $118: shares called away at $110; total (110−100) × 100 + 200 = +$1,200 — but you surrendered $800 of upside beyond $110.
At expiry S = $88: shares lose $1,200, premium refunds $200 → net −$1,000.

Lesson: a covered call does not protect the downside — it trades capped upside for certain income.

EXAMPLE 3 · PROTECTIVE PUT Insuring a $10,000 position

Hold 100 shares (cost $100). Buy 1 put, three months out, K = $95, premium $2.50 (pay $250).

S crashes to $70: shares lose $3,000, but the put is worth (95−70) × 100 = $2,500 → loss capped at −$750 ($500 gap + $250 premium).
S rallies to $120: shares gain $2,000, put expires → net +$1,750.

Lesson: insurance costs are certain and recurring (~2.5% per quarter here); buying it permanently drags returns badly — use it around event windows or at extended highs.

EXAMPLE 4 · BULL CALL SPREAD Let the short leg pay half your cost

Expecting a mild rise toward $110: buy 1 K = $100 call (pay $4) and sell 1 K = $110 call (collect $1.50); net cost $2.50 ($250), breakeven $102.50.

At expiry S ≥ $110: full spread earned, (110−100) × 100 − 250 = +$750 (+300%).
At expiry S = $105: (105−100) × 100 − 250 = +$250.
At expiry S ≤ $100: −$250 (versus −$400 on the naked call).

Lesson: the short $110 leg cuts cost from $400 to $250 and pulls breakeven from $104 to $102.50 — in exchange for giving up everything above $110. If $110 was your target anyway, that giveaway is free.

EXAMPLE 5 · CASH-SECURED PUT Want to buy at $95? Get paid while you wait

You planned a limit buy at $95. Instead: sell 1 put, one month out, K = $95, premium $2 (+$200), keeping $9,500 in reserve for assignment.

At expiry S ≥ $95: the put dies; you keep $200 (~2.1% for the month) — no shares, but the waiting itself got paid. Sell next month's put and repeat.
At expiry S = $88: assigned 100 shares at $95; effective cost $95 − 2 = $93 — $2 cheaper than your original limit order; the $500 paper loss matches what the limit order would have produced anyway.

Once assigned, turn around and sell covered calls (Example 2); when called away, go back to selling puts — that cycle is the Wheel.

Lesson: a cash-secured put is a paid limit order. It has exactly one precondition: you would honestly be glad to own this stock at K. Collecting premium on a stock you don't want to own is just naked selling in disguise.

EXAMPLE 6 · STRADDLE INTO EARNINGS: IV CRUSH LIVE Right on direction, wrong on the money

The night before earnings, IV is pumped to 60%: the K = $100 call and put cost $4 each; the straddle costs $8 ($800), breakevens $92 / $108.

Earnings "beat"; S jumps to $105 (+5%) — but IV collapses to 30%: the call is worth ≈ $5.50, the put ≈ $0.50, total $6 → −$200. Direction and movement were both fine; volatility took the money.
Had S reached $112: call worth $12+, straddle ≈ $12.50 → +$450 — only a move beyond the breakevens, i.e. beyond expectations, pays.

Lesson: pre-earnings premiums already pre-charge the expected move (about ±8% here). A straddle is not a bet that the stock will move — it's a bet that it will move more than everyone already expects. Flip it around, and that is exactly how sellers (iron condors) harvest the IV crush after the event.

OPTIONS DISCIPLINE FOR BEGINNERS

① Money in long options is always sized as if it will go to zero — at most ~5% of the account; ② learn in this order: covered calls and protective puts (backed by stock) → spreads → outright directional buys; ③ never sell naked options; ④ before every order, write the three answers: max loss, max gain, breakeven; ⑤ option mechanics (exercise style, margin, settlement) differ sharply by market — your local market's official rules govern.

ONE LAST THING

Technical analysis is, at bottom, a graphical record of crowd behavior. Markets change endlessly; greed, fear, herding, and regret do not — that constancy is the entire reason technical analysis keeps working. The tools are only tools: discipline and money management are what decide the long run.
